# Who Needs AAC After Post-Stroke Aphasia: A Clinical Synthesis

**Date:** 2026-04-05
**Sources:** Literature in `literature/mkv/` — all claims drawn from full-text MKVs
**Purpose:** Background research for PhD proposal; characterising the AAC candidacy population

---

## 1. Epidemiology

About one-third of stroke patients develop aphasia. Beukelman (2020, Ch. 15) reports that "the median proportion of stroke patients described as having aphasia is 34%," and that approximately 100,000 individuals discharged from US hospitals annually with aphasia have had a left-hemisphere cerebrovascular accident. Koul (2011, Ch. 1) reports prevalence of "about 66 per 100,000 people in the United Kingdom" and notes that global prevalence will increase as populations age.

The epidemiological fact that drives AAC candidacy: **up to 40% of all people with aphasia will experience chronic, severe language impairments across modalities** (Beukelman, 2020, Ch. 15). This is the core population for whom AAC is clinically indicated.

Age profile: "most people who have aphasia acquire it after the age of 60 years" (Beukelman, 2020), though "a substantial number of younger adults can also develop aphasia while in the prime of their school-aged or working years." In the AAC-specific studies reviewed by Koul (2011, Ch. 4), the mean participant age was 63.73 years, with a range of 27–86 years.

Gender: roughly equal in the reviewed studies — 48% male, 52% female (Koul, 2011, Ch. 4).

## 2. Aphasia Types and Their Relation to AAC Need

### 2.1 Taxonomy

Koul (2011, Ch. 2) provides the standard taxonomy, organised around the Boston/WAB classification. The clinically important distinction for AAC is **fluent vs. nonfluent**:

- **Nonfluent aphasias** (anterior/left frontal lesions): Broca's, transcortical motor (TCMA), global. Characterised by telegraphic/agrammatic speech, reduced phrase length, effortful output, and relatively preserved (or mildly impaired) auditory comprehension.
- **Fluent aphasias** (posterior lesions): Wernicke's, transcortical sensory (TCSA), conduction, anomic. Characterised by paraphasias, jargon, neologisms, and often severely impaired comprehension, but with preserved output fluency.

### 2.2 Aphasia Type Mapped to AAC Profile

Beukelman (2020, Ch. 15) maps aphasia types directly onto AAC communicator categories:

| Aphasia type | Fluency | Comprehension | Repetition | AAC communicator category |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Global | Severely nonfluent | Severely impaired | Severely impaired | Emerging / contextual choice |
| Broca's | Nonfluent (agrammatic) | Mild–moderate impairment | Impaired | Contextual choice → generative |
| Wernicke's | Fluent (jargon) | Severely impaired | Impaired | Contextual choice; needs augmented input |
| Transcortical motor | Nonfluent | Mild impairment | Preserved | Transitional → stored-message |
| Transcortical sensory | Fluent | Severely impaired | Preserved | Heavily partner-dependent; augmented input |
| Conduction | Fluent | Mild–moderate impairment | Severely impaired | Stored-message → generative |
| Anomic | Fluent | Preserved | Relatively preserved | Specific-need / generative |

Koul (2011, Ch. 3) adds that "independent communicators may depict characteristics of persons with moderate to severe Broca's aphasia, transcortical motor aphasia, or conduction aphasia." People with almost any aphasia type can be specific-need communicators if their communication is mostly functional but breaks down in high-demand situations.

## 3. The Garrett–Lasker–Beukelman Communicator Classification

All sources converge on this six-level framework (Garrett & Lasker, 2005; Garrett & Beukelman, 1992) as the clinical standard for AAC candidacy and system selection. It appears in detail in Beukelman (2020, Ch. 15), Koul (2011, Ch. 3), and CAYA/Lowis (2024).

### Partner-Dependent Communicators

**1. Emerging Communicator**
The most severely impaired group. Koul (2011, Ch. 3): "primarily depict characteristics of persons with global aphasia. They demonstrate severe deficits in all aspects of language (i.e., spontaneous speech, auditory comprehension, reading and writing)."

Beukelman (2020): "Emerging AAC communicators may have both severe aphasia and apraxia of speech. They have extreme difficulties speaking, using symbols, and responding to conversational input... they do not easily associate photos or line drawings of common objects with their referents."

CAYA/Lowis (2024) characterises their challenges: "Poor comprehension without visual or personal context; Inconsistent or nonexistent yes/no response; No functional speech or gestures."

**2. Contextual Choice Communicator**
Can point to written word choices, photos, or symbols when provided by a communication partner. Beukelman (2020): "contextual choice AAC communicators do not have the linguistic competence to initiate or add to a conversation on their own... they can participate in topical conversations when communication partners provide written or pictorial choices on a turn-by-turn basis." The primary tool is the written choice conversation technique.

Key research finding from Beukelman (2020): "many individuals with severe aphasia have demonstrated an ability to participate in interesting conversations when partners implement the written choice conversation support — despite very limited reading comprehension test scores... People with aphasia with minimal reading ability on formal tests were more than 90% accurate when responding to conversational questions by pointing to written choices, as long as the choices were presented within the context of a conversation and spoken aloud by the partner while he or she wrote them."

CAYA/Lowis (2024) challenges: "Speech or gestures are minimally comprehensible; Generate little information on their own; Seldom initiates, even by pointing or vocalizing; Poor comprehension of decontextualized auditory information."

**3. Transitional Communicator**
Can access stored messages with cueing from a partner. Koul (2011, Ch. 3): "Transitional communicators are able to point to symbols or words and can use gestures to augment their spoken language... depends on cues and prompts to answer questions and stay engaged in a spontaneous conversation."

Beukelman (2020): "individuals with global aphasia diagnoses or frontal lobe involvement have the most difficulty transitioning to self-initiated communication." The barrier is often initiation, not access ability per se.

### Partner-Independent Communicators

**4. Stored-Message Communicator**
Independently accesses pre-stored messages without partner cueing. Beukelman (2020): "They have enough executive function and initiation ability to learn to consistently access entire phrase or sentence messages in specific contexts... stored-message AAC communicators can learn to initiate and use their systems to supplement or substitute for speech without prompting within familiar situations."

CAYA/Lowis (2024) requires: "Ability to read at the word/phrase/sentence level; Memory and attention sufficient for navigational pathways; Navigation ability — Number of levels on a display."

**5. Generative Message Communicator**
Can generate novel messages using letters, symbols, or partial words. Beukelman (2020): "generative AAC communicators... are able to formulate messages using symbols or text and can use a combination of aided and unaided supports... Their semantic networks are usually more complete... and they can generate some grammatically and semantically complex utterances."

Typical aphasia profiles: Broca's, anomic, mild conduction aphasia.

**6. Specific-Need Communicator**
Communication is mostly functional but breaks down in particular high-demand situations. Koul (2011, Ch. 3): "Persons with mild aphasia who have difficulty in specific situations because of clarity and specificity of information required in those situations." CAYA/Lowis (2024): "may not need AAC for general communication but uses AAC for specific high-demand situations (e.g., phone calls, medical appointments)."

People with almost any aphasia type can be specific-need communicators. TouchChat (2024) lists examples: using a Keyword and Calendar page for scheduling and appointment communication.

## 4. AAC Candidacy Criteria and Assessment Approaches

### 4.1 No Exclusionary Criteria Based on Timing or Severity

Tobii (tobii-aphasia-therapy-guide.md) states explicitly: AAC applies to "anyone with aphasia, any time post their onset, at any level of language impairment." Using AAC "will not hinder PWA's ability to regain speech. Instead, it can help PWA get back into their social circles and daily activities helping to prevent depression."

### 4.2 The MCST-A as Primary Assessment Tool

The Multimodal Communication Screening Task for Persons with Aphasia (MCST-A; Garrett & Lasker, 2005) is the central clinical instrument. Beukelman (2020): "Research suggests that the MCST-A effectively distinguishes between communicators who require partner support to indicate choices and stored-message AAC communicators who can independently search through the booklet to locate symbols." It also distinguishes generative communicators who can combine symbols in logical sequences.

### 4.3 The Five Core Assessment Questions (CAYA Framework)

CAYA/Lowis (2024) operationalises candidacy into five questions:

1. Does the PWA use unaided communication strategies (gestures, writing, drawing)? With what degree of cueing?
2. Are partner-supported strategies being used?
3. Can the PWA use external stored information/messages on a communication board?
4. Can the PWA access stored messages on a speech generating device?
5. Can the PWA formulate/generate messages to communicate?

Beukelman (2020, Table 15.5) frames these identically: unaided modalities; partner-supported techniques; external stored information; stored messages via speech output; generative messages through speech output.

### 4.4 Standardised Battery Input

Beukelman (2020) recommends the Western Aphasia Battery-Revised (WAB-R), Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Examination, or Boston Assessment of Severe Aphasia to characterise severity and modality profile before AAC system selection. CAYA/Lowis (2024) also references the ALA (Assessment for Living with Aphasia).

### 4.5 AAC Trials and Role Play

Beukelman (2020): "To evaluate how effectively a communicator can access stored messages on a speech-output system in an actual communication situation, the AAC team can develop scripted scenarios representing a community transaction and program the communicator's AAC technology with relevant messages." The degree of cueing required — none, minimal, moderate, maximal — is a key candidacy indicator.

## 5. Literacy, Comprehension, and Symbolic Ability

### 5.1 Literacy is an Enabling Factor, Not an Exclusion Criterion

Formal reading test scores should not be used as a barrier to AAC candidacy. The written-choice strategy works even for people with near-absent formal reading scores, provided choices are presented contextually and spoken aloud by the partner (Beukelman, 2020). Koul (2011, Ch. 5): "the written-choice communication strategy is only helpful if the person with aphasia possesses sufficient residual reading and auditory processing abilities" — but "sufficient" is defined functionally, not by test score.

Higher literacy expands AAC options toward the generative end of the continuum.

### 5.2 Literacy Requirements by Communicator Level

CAYA/Lowis (2024) stratifies literacy requirements:

| Communicator type | Literacy requirement |
|---|---|
| Emerging | Word recognition as asset; typically very limited |
| Contextual choice | Recognise a word in context; read at word or phrase level in context; identify first letter |
| Transitional | Recognise personally relevant text; write a single word to answer a question |
| Stored-message | Read at word/phrase/sentence level |
| Generative | Recognise items through whole-word or phonological strategies |
| Specific-need | Variable; generally functional literacy intact |

TouchChat (2024): "The Keyboard & Calendar Page is for people who have aphasia and some literacy skills."

Beukelman (2020) notes that spelling for generative AAC "is often extremely difficult for people with aphasia" because it requires sequencing arbitrary symbols — a double burden of motor sequencing and phonological mapping under impaired language.

### 5.3 Comprehension Requirements by Communicator Level

CAYA/Lowis (2024):

- Emerging: at minimum, reliability of yes/no
- Contextual choice: comprehension of written words, pictures, or other visual symbols in shared context
- Transitional: comprehension of verbal information with visual support
- Stored-message: ability to understand questions out of context; ability to understand conversation between 2+ people
- Generative: comprehension of abstract and decontextualised language

People with fluent aphasias (Wernicke's, TCSA) have particular comprehension difficulties and need **augmented input supports**: partner gestures, writes keywords, or draws while speaking to make incoming language accessible.

### 5.4 Symbolic Ability Assessment

CAYA/Lowis (2024): "Assess symbolic ability — what level of representation client can use (i.e. objects, photos, picture symbols, text)." Tools: Test of Aided Symbol Performance (TASP) and MCST-A.

TouchChat (2024): "In most instances, personal photographs are easier to identify and relate to than symbols."

## 6. Cognitive Requirements

### 6.1 Cognition is Separable from Language

Beukelman (2020): "some people with aphasia demonstrate relatively intact intellectual ability; others demonstrate reductions in processing speed, attention, memory, executive functions, and/or problem solving that cannot be completely explained by language impairments alone." Assessment with the Cognitive Linguistic Quick Test Plus (CLQT+; Helm-Estabrooks, 2017) is recommended — it "contains several subtests that assess attention, memory, and reasoning without taxing linguistic ability."

### 6.2 Executive Function and Self-Initiation

Nicholas et al. (2005), cited in Koul (2011, Ch. 4), found executive function was a key variable in computer-based AAC use. Beukelman (2020): "Helm-Estabrooks (2002) suggested that several nonverbal reasoning tasks (e.g., trail making) may be useful in identifying individuals with cognitive ability to use high-tech, symbol-based AAC."

People with frontal lobe involvement have the most difficulty transitioning from partner-dependent to partner-independent AAC use.

### 6.3 Working Memory and Navigation

Beukelman (2020): "arranging messages in AAC systems with multiple displays... may impose significant processing challenges for many people with aphasia who demonstrate problems recalling the location of stored messages and symbols. They must demonstrate sufficient working memory to complete the steps involved in accessing the messages before forgetting their intent."

This is why low-tech, single-display options are sometimes preferable to multi-level dynamic displays — visibility and immediacy of access reduce working memory load.

CAYA/Lowis (2024) formally assesses: "Memory and attention sufficient for navigational pathways; Navigation ability — Number of levels on a display."

### 6.4 Cognitive Flexibility

Purdy & Koch (2006), cited in Beukelman (2020), correlated the ability to shift between multiple communication modalities with higher levels of cognitive flexibility. Independent AAC communicators need metacognitive awareness of communication breakdowns and strategic flexibility to switch modalities.

## 7. The Role of Co-Occurring Apraxia of Speech

Beukelman (2020): "Apraxia of speech typically co-occurs with anterior aphasia syndromes (e.g. Broca's aphasia, global aphasia, transcortical motor aphasia)." Apraxia of speech (AOS) compounds expressive difficulty because it disrupts the motor execution of even residual speech, gestures, and head nods.

The case of Robert (contextual choice communicator, Beukelman, 2020): "because of apraxia, however, he also required tactile cues and a model for approximately 2 months until he could produce consistent [yes/no] responses."

Koul (2011, Ch. 2) notes that limb apraxia — common alongside AOS — can interfere with pointing, gesturing, and device operation. Beukelman (2020): "they may experience difficulties when asked to complete a complex sequence of motor movements (e.g., locating words on multiple pages of a book) because of limb apraxia or cognitive deficits."

## 8. Predictors of AAC Success

### 8.1 Partner Support (Strongest Predictor for Severe Aphasia)

Beukelman (2020): "research shows that when a PWA has at least one close supporting person they will have a significantly greater chance of successfully living with aphasia" (citing Simmons-Mackie et al., 2010). "Failure to identify and adequately prepare communication partners is a common reason for unsuccessful AAC intervention among people with severe aphasia."

Tobii (tobii-aphasia-therapy-guide.md): "Another important aspect of making the person with aphasia successful is to involve family members and/or caregivers as much as possible."

### 8.2 Motivation and Communication Initiative

Beukelman (2020) documents the case of Marco (stored-message communicator): the barrier to success was not ability but shame — "he was ashamed of using the machine in public." Tobii (tobii-aphasia-therapy-guide.md) lists "increased initiation of communication" as a key success indicator. CAYA/Lowis (2024): "communicative intent; motivation to communicate a message" is listed as a target skill for stored-message communicators.

### 8.3 Personalisation and Topic Relevance

Fox, Sohlberg, and Fried-Oken (2001), cited in Beukelman (2020): "one individual... communicated much more extensively about topics that he had selected than those that had been chosen for him."

Tobii (tobii-aphasia-therapy-guide.md): "One of the keys to making the tools found in the Aphasia Pages functional for your client is to personalize it when you can."

TouchChat (2024): "There is no standardized vocabulary file that will meet even the minimum needs of every person with aphasia... Customization is the responsibility of the speech language pathologist."

### 8.4 Time Post-Onset Does Not Predict Failure

Van de Sandt-Koenderman et al. (2005), cited in Koul (2011, Ch. 4): "Time post-onset brain injury had no impact on successful outcome" and notably "older participants tended to be more successful in using PCAD than younger participants." Tobii: tools can be used "any time post their onset."

### 8.5 Appropriate System Match

Beukelman (2020) identifies documented reasons for AAC failure:
1. Vocabulary and content do not match specific real-life communication opportunities
2. Important communication partners are not willing to accept AAC
3. AAC technology is too large/unwieldy or too small for the person's vision capabilities
4. The individual's social networks have become so limited that there are few opportunities to communicate

### 8.6 Display Density and Navigation Complexity

Petroi, Koul, & Corwin (2014), cited in Beukelman (2020): "people with aphasia identify symbols within an AAC device most readily when fewer symbols per screen are displayed and when minimal navigation is required."

Wallace and Hux (2013), cited in Beukelman (2020): found "improved efficiency of access and accuracy of learning when provided with a navigation ring" compared to standard multiple-display navigation.

### 8.7 Concrete Over Abstract Symbols

Beck & Fritz (1998), reviewed in Koul (2011, Ch. 4): "across all participants, a significantly fewer number of iconic codes were learned for abstract messages than for concrete messages." This effect was stronger for Wernicke's aphasia than Broca's aphasia profiles.

### 8.8 Symbol Combination Ability by Aphasia Type

Koul (2011, Ch. 4): "all participants with Broca's aphasia and one participant with global aphasia were able to identify and combine graphic symbols to produce sentences of varying degrees of syntactical complexity. One participant with global aphasia was not able to produce any sentences correctly." This suggests Broca's profiles respond better to generative symbol-combination tasks than global profiles.

## 10. Clinical Profile Summary: Who Needs AAC After Stroke

**Who needs AAC:** Any person with aphasia severe enough to prevent functional communication through speech alone. Core population: global, severe Broca's, severe Wernicke's, severe transcortical motor aphasia, and/or co-occurring severe apraxia of speech. Up to 40% of all PWA will have chronic severe impairments qualifying them for AAC (Beukelman, 2020).

**Timing:** From the very start of intervention — "begin using AAC from the start of intervention" (Tobii). AAC is not a last resort; it supports speech recovery rather than replacing it.

**The determining factor is not aphasia type alone** but the functional communicator profile across five dimensions:
1. Unaided expressive ability (speech, gesture, writing, drawing)
2. Receptive/comprehension ability (auditory, visual, contextual)
3. Symbolic/representational ability (objects → photos → line drawings → text)
4. Ability to use stored or generative messages
5. Cognitive/executive capacity for navigation

These map onto the six Garrett–Lasker communicator types.

**Literacy and comprehension** are enabling factors, not exclusion criteria. Even those with near-absent functional reading can use AAC via contextual support and spoken presentation of choices. Higher literacy enables higher-tech and more generative systems.

**Cognitive factors** determine viable system complexity — from simple object pointing (emerging) to multi-level dynamic display with word prediction (generative). Assessed through non-linguistic tasks where possible (CLQT+, trail making).

**Success predictors:** trained and motivated communication partners; personally relevant vocabulary; motivation and acceptance by the person with aphasia; system complexity matched to current ability; regular community-based practice; ongoing vocabulary updating as needs and abilities change.

## 11. Relevance to This Project

The participant in this project — an Afrikaans-speaking man, post-stroke, intact comprehension, able to read, using a touchscreen tablet — maps to the **stored-message to generative** range of the Garrett–Lasker continuum. Intact comprehension and literacy are significant assets that place him above the contextual-choice level and make personalised, touchscreen-based symbol-and-text AAC fully viable.

Key design implications from the literature:
- Fewer symbols per screen, minimal navigation, concrete over abstract symbols
- Personally relevant vocabulary (his topics, his people, his daily routines)
- Family/caregiver involvement and training is as important as the technology itself
- System must support communication initiation, not just response to partner prompts
- Customisation is not optional — no standard vocabulary set meets aphasia users' needs

### 11.1 Large Buttons and Minimal Navigation

Beyond the aphasia-specific literature, two additional user factors justify the large-button, low-density design:

- **Limited fine motor control** — post-stroke motor impairment affects precision touch, making small targets unreliable regardless of cognitive load.
- **No prior touchscreen familiarity** — the participant has never been a technology user; touchscreens are not a natural interaction mode for him. Large, unambiguous targets reduce the interaction barrier imposed by unfamiliar hardware.

These factors compound the aphasia-specific argument for low-density displays (§8.6). Even for family members without aphasia or motor impairment, the large-button layout is easier to use — suggesting the design is robust rather than over-accommodating.

### 11.2 Why Not a Keyboard?

A keyboard appears to solve the problem: unlimited expressive range, no vocabulary curation, familiar metaphor. But it is the wrong tool for aphasia specifically.

**The keyboard problem is linguistic, not motor.** The stroke damaged the language production system itself. Typing requires:

1. Lexical retrieval — finding the word (often impaired)
2. Phonological encoding — mapping the word to its letter sequence (often impaired)
3. Sequential symbol production — letter by letter under impaired language (working memory burden)

Beukelman (2020) states this directly: spelling for generative AAC "is often extremely difficult for people with aphasia because it requires sequencing arbitrary symbols — a double burden of motor sequencing and phonological mapping under impaired language."

**Pre-stored messages bypass the damaged system.** Tapping a labelled button is *recognition*, not *production*. Recognition uses a different and typically more preserved pathway than language generation. This is why people with near-absent formal reading scores can still be 90%+ accurate when choosing from written options presented in context (§5.1) — they are recognising, not constructing.

**The trade-off is real and must be managed.** A keyboard offers unlimited range; stored messages are bounded by what has been programmed. Vocabulary mismatch is a documented AAC failure mode (§8.5). This is why ongoing customisation — building vocabulary around his actual life, people, and routines — is not optional. The stored-message approach only works if the stored messages cover what he actually needs to say.

**The system can evolve.** If language recovers further, the design can shift toward more generative features (partial word completion, free-text input as a supplementary option). The current stored-message implementation is the right starting point, not a permanent ceiling.

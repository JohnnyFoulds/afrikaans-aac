# Caregiver Communication Guide — For Francisca (Mom)

**Date:** 2026-04-03
**Purpose:** Practical guide for Mom on communicating effectively with Dad using the AAC app. Based on the Supported Conversation for Adults with Aphasia (SCA™) framework developed by the Aphasia Institute, and Odendaal et al. (2024) findings on AAC in South African home contexts.

**This is NOT a medical document.** It is a practical, plain-language guide written for a family caregiver without clinical training.

---

## What Dad has — and what he hasn't lost

Dad has **aphasia** — a communication disorder caused by his stroke. It affects his ability to speak and sometimes to find words when writing. It does **not** affect his intelligence, his memory of people and events, his feelings, or his personality.

He knows what he wants to say. He just cannot always get the words out.

The app gives him a way to say things by tapping. When he taps a button, the tablet speaks the phrase aloud. **He chooses — the tablet speaks.**

---

## The three most important things

### 1. He understands you

Speak to him normally. Do not slow down excessively or use a childish tone. He is not confused — he simply cannot speak back easily. Treat him the way you would want to be treated if you suddenly lost the ability to talk.

### 2. Give him time

After you ask him something, wait. It may take him longer than usual to find the right button or to show you what he means. Do not answer for him. Silence is not awkward — it is him working. Your patience tells him you want to hear what he has to say.

### 3. The app is his voice — not a toy

When he taps a button and the tablet speaks, that is him speaking. Respond to it the way you would respond to him saying it directly. "Ek is honger" means he is hungry. Respond accordingly.

---

## The IN–OUT–VERIFY method (SCA™)

This is the core of Supported Conversation for Adults with Aphasia. Use these three steps in every interaction.

### IN — Make sure he understands you

When you need him to understand something important:
- Say it clearly and short ("Wil jy tee hê?")
- Back it up with a gesture — point to the cup, mime drinking
- Write the key word on paper if needed ("TEE?")
- Show him choices in writing ("TEE" or "KOFFIE") and let him point

Do not assume he didn't understand. He may have understood perfectly but not been able to respond yet.

### OUT — Help him express himself

When he wants to say something:
- First let him try on the app
- If he is struggling to find the right button, say: "Wys my" — let him point at something
- Offer yes/no questions to narrow it down: "Is dit oor pyn?" → "Oor kos?"
- Never guess out loud and move on — always verify

### VERIFY — Make sure you got it right

Once you think you understand:
- Say it back to him: "Jy sê jy is honger — klop dit?"
- Wait for him to confirm (nod, yes button, or shake his head)
- If it is critical information (pain, medical), do not act until confirmed

## Using the app together

### Starting a conversation

When you want to know how he is or what he needs:
1. Hold the tablet so he can reach it easily
2. Ask: "Hoe voel jy?" or "Het jy iets nodig?"
3. Wait for him to navigate to a category and tap a phrase
4. Respond to what the app says as if he said it himself

### When he presses the emergency button

The red **Noodgeval** button is always visible on every screen. If he presses it, go to him immediately — he does not press it lightly.

The **Roep vir Francisca** button on every screen does the same. If you hear the tablet say your name, he needs you.

### When he taps something you didn't expect

Do not correct him or say "no that's wrong". He chose that button intentionally. Use VERIFY: "Jy sê jy is bang — praat ons hieroor?" Even if it seems surprising, take it seriously.

### When he gets frustrated

Signs of frustration: he may put the tablet down, shake his head, or look away. This is normal.
- Acknowledge it: "Ek weet dit is moeilik. Ek is hier."
- Take a break — do not push
- Try again in a few minutes with a simpler approach (yes/no questions)

Every time a communication attempt succeeds — even a small one — it makes the next attempt more likely to succeed. You are not just solving the moment; you are building his confidence and his habit of using the app. Persistence is what makes this work over time.

## A note for you, Mom

This guide is full of things to do and ways to help Dad. But none of it works unless you are also looked after.

What has happened to Dad has also happened to you. The person you have known for decades now communicates differently. That is a loss, and it is normal to grieve it — even while he is still here, even while you are doing everything right. Speech therapists who work with aphasia families say that caregivers who are able to acknowledge that grief, rather than push through it alone, are the ones who can sustain the patience and effort that this kind of communication requires.

You do not have to be strong all the time. There will be days when this is too hard. That is not failure — it is human.

A few things that help:

- Tell Johannes when something is not working or when you are struggling
- Use the "Laat my rus" button on the app — if Dad needs rest, you need rest too
- Know that the small moments where communication works are real victories, even if the day was hard overall

You are not a speech therapist. You are his wife. That is exactly who he needs.

## Practical day-to-day habits

**Morning check-in:**
Every morning, hold the tablet for him and ask: "Hoe voel jy vandag?" Let him answer with the Ek voel category. This gives him a voice first thing in the day.

**Before meals:**
Ask: "Wat wil jy hê?" and open the Ek het nodig category. Give him a chance to choose.

**Visitors:**
When someone visits who doesn't know about his condition, show them the "Ek het 'n beroerte gehad" category first. He can tap "Ek het 'n beroerte gehad" and "Ek kan nie praat nie, maar ek verstaan alles" to explain himself. This prevents the visitor from talking over him or about him as if he is not there.

**Bedtime:**
Ask: "Is jy gemaklik?" and let him confirm or use the pain/needs categories.

## What NOT to do

| Avoid | Rather |
| --- | --- |
| Finishing his sentences or tapping buttons for him | Let him navigate and choose himself |
| Speaking about him to others while he is in the room | Include him: "Pa, ek verduidelik vir x — klop dit?" |
| Saying "never mind" when he struggles | Stay patient and use the IN–OUT–VERIFY method |
| Responding to the tablet as if it is a machine | Respond as if he spoke the words himself |
| Over-praising every interaction ("Goed gedoen!") | Just respond naturally |
| Rushing him because others are waiting | Ask others to wait. His communication matters. |

## If the app doesn't have the phrase he needs

He will show you. He may point, look at something, make a gesture, or try to write. When this happens:
1. Take out a pen and paper
2. Write options and let him point to one
3. Note what he was trying to say — tell Johannes so the phrase can be added

The app will grow over time. Every new phrase that gets added is a new thing he can say.

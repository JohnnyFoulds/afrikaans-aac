## Frequency-ranked quick phrase bank

The design space is small and well-trodden. The substantive decisions are:

**Scoring function.** A pure frequency count $f(p) = |\text{uses}(p)|$ is the naïve baseline and behaves badly: phrases used heavily in week one will permanently dominate even after the user's vocabulary drifts. The standard remedy is recency-weighted frequency with exponential decay,

$$
s(p, t_\text{now}) = \sum_{t_i \in \text{uses}(p)} e^{-\lambda (t_\text{now} - t_i)}
$$

where $\lambda$ tunes the half-life. A half-life of roughly 7–30 days is a defensible starting range for AAC; shorter half-lives make the bank feel responsive but volatile, longer half-lives make it feel stable but stale. This is structurally equivalent to LRFU [Lee et al., *IEEE Trans. Computers*, 2001] and to forgetting-curve formulations from spaced-repetition literature. Cheaper to implement is an exponentially weighted moving average updated on each use, which avoids storing the full usage log.

**Granularity.** What counts as "a phrase"? The cleanest definition is *the exact string the user submitted to TTS*, hashed and counted verbatim. Splitting into n-grams or sub-phrases sounds clever but invites two failure modes: (i) the bank fills with high-frequency fragments ("turn the", "I want to") that are not useful as one-tap utterances, and (ii) you lose the user's authorial intent. The whole-utterance approach is also the only one that maps cleanly onto whole-utterance audio caching, which matters for the second problem below.

**Near-duplicate handling.** "pass me my protein shake" and "pass my protein shake" should probably collapse. Cheap options: Levenshtein distance with a normalised threshold, or embedding cosine similarity using a small sentence-transformer (e.g. `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`) with thresholding. The latter is overkill at small scale but generalises if you later want semantic clustering of the bank.

**Cold start and pinning.** The bank must be useable on day one. Seed it with a generic AAC corpus (greetings, requests, yes/no, pain/comfort scales) and provide explicit user pinning so essentials never get evicted regardless of score. Without pinning, the algorithm will eventually displace a low-frequency but critical utterance such as "I need my medication."

A SQLite table `(utterance_hash, utterance_text, last_used_ts, score, pinned)` with an index on `score DESC` and a top-K query is sufficient. This is genuinely a weekend's work.

## TTS word-stitching

This approach has been tried — for about three decades, from roughly 1980 to the mid-2010s — and was abandoned for sound reasons. What is being proposed is essentially **unit-selection concatenative synthesis** at word granularity, which is the coarsest and worst-performing variant. The technical objections are not stylistic preference; they are acoustic:

1. **Prosody collapse.** A word spoken in isolation carries citation-form intonation: flat or falling pitch, fully articulated, with terminal lengthening. Concatenating ten such words produces a sequence of ten micro-utterances, each ending as if it were the end of a sentence. The listener perceives this as severely robotic, even when the underlying voice quality is high. This is *the* reason neural TTS replaced concatenative TTS — not because the unit audio was bad, but because no amount of unit-level quality compensates for absent sentence-level prosody.

2. **Coarticulation loss.** Phoneme boundaries between adjacent words in natural speech blend (the /t/ in "want to" reduces, the /d/ in "did you" palatalises). Word-level stitching eliminates all of this and produces over-articulated speech that sounds non-native or pathological.

3. **Boundary artefacts.** Concatenation at word boundaries produces clicks, energy discontinuities, and unnatural silences unless aggressive cross-fading and pitch-matching are applied — at which point you have reinvented half of a 2005-era TTS engine.

4. **The cost argument is mostly wrong.** Modern TTS APIs charge per character, not per request. Caching individual words to save cost yields trivial savings (because words are short) while incurring all the quality penalties above. The only legitimate motivations for caching are **latency** and **offline operation**, neither of which requires word-level granularity.

The correct architecture for an AAC system is some combination of the following, ordered by what I would actually build:

- **On-device neural TTS as the default path.** Web Speech API in browsers, `AVSpeechSynthesizer` on iOS/macOS, `TextToSpeech` on Android, or [Piper](https://github.com/rhasspy/piper) for embedded/desktop deployments. Quality is now genuinely good (Piper's `en_US-lessac-medium` voice is hard to distinguish from cloud TTS in casual use), latency is sub-100 ms, cost is zero, and it works offline. This alone may end the problem.
- **Whole-utterance audio caching.** When the user submits a phrase to TTS, hash the text and cache the resulting audio blob keyed by hash. The quick-phrase bank entries are then served from cache after first use — instant playback, no synthesis delay, no quality compromise, no concatenation. This is the *correct* form of the caching intuition.
- **Cloud TTS as a premium tier**, optionally, using ElevenLabs / OpenAI / AWS Polly Neural for a more expressive voice, again with whole-utterance caching.

If for some reason on-device TTS is unacceptable and per-request cloud cost dominates the design, the granularity to cache is whole utterances or, at worst, whole prosodic phrases (clauses), never words.

## Why word-stitching is *worse* for Afrikaans specifically

Beyond the general objections to concatenative synthesis raised previously, Afrikaans presents three language-specific problems that make word-level stitching particularly destructive:

1. **Phonemic vowel length.** Afrikaans contrasts long and short vowels in minimal pairs (*maan* /ɑː/ "moon" vs *man* /a/ "man"; *kies* /i/ "choose" vs *kis* /ə/ "chest"). Vowel duration in connected speech is conditioned on adjacent segments and prosodic position; word-isolated citation forms over-lengthen all vowels uniformly, blurring the contrast.

2. **The voiceless velar/uvular fricative /x/** (the "g" in *gaan*, *berg*) exhibits substantial coarticulation with following vowels. Stitched at word boundaries, /x/-initial words after vowel-final words produce a perceptible glottal break absent in natural Afrikaans.

3. **Productive compounding.** Afrikaans, like Dutch and German, freely forms long single-word compounds — *lewensversekeringsmaatskappy* ("life insurance company"), *kindertehuis*, *koffiebeker* — that are single prosodic units with one primary stress. The compounding is so productive that the unit you would cache is not "the word" but "this morphemic combination", and the cache hit rate collapses. Any morpheme-level stitching strategy produces obviously wrong stress placement.

These are not subjective objections; they are categorial features of the phonology [6].

## The correct architecture: hybrid with whole-utterance caching

The intuition behind caching is right; the granularity is wrong. The architecture below preserves the cost and offline benefits being chased without the prosody collapse:

```
                ┌─────────────────────────────────┐
                │  User submits utterance U       │
                └────────────┬────────────────────┘
                             │
                  hash(U) → key
                             │
                ┌────────────▼────────────┐
                │  Audio cache lookup     │
                │  SQLite or filesystem   │ ◄── grows monotonically with use
                └────────────┬────────────┘
                             │
                ┌────────────┴────────────┐
                │                         │
              HIT                       MISS
                │                         │
                │              ┌──────────┴──────────┐
                │              │                     │
                │            ONLINE                OFFLINE
                │              │                     │
                │     Azure Edge-TTS af-ZA    MMS-TTS-afr local
                │              │                     │
                │      cache(key, audio)      cache(key, audio)
                │              │                     │
                └──────────────▼─────────────────────▼
                          Play audio
```

Properties:

- **Cost.** Each unique utterance pays the cloud cost exactly once; the *N*-th repetition is free. For an AAC user whose 50–500-utterance vocabulary stabilises within weeks, lifetime cloud cost is bounded and small.
- **Offline operation.** Anything previously said is available offline at the original quality. Genuinely novel utterances spoken offline fall back to MMS-TTS — degraded but functional. When the device next reconnects, a background re-synthesis pass can replace MMS-rendered audio in the cache with Azure-rendered audio for any utterance whose `tts_source = 'mms'` and `play_count > k`, transparently improving cached quality over time.
- **Latency.** Cache hits are sub-50 ms playback; cache misses online are 200–500 ms; cache misses offline are MMS inference time (~real-time on a modern CPU for short utterances).
- **Shared key with the phrase bank.** The quick-phrase bank described in the previous turn keys on the same utterance hash. Phrase-bank entries are by construction cache hits — instant playback, no synthesis delay, no quality compromise, no concatenation.

## On word-by-word speech during composition

The hypothesis ("might sound more fluent if one word is spoken as it is selected") conflates two distinct things that have opposite answers in the AAC literature.

### (a) Listener-facing word-by-word output: avoid

Speaking each word to the conversational partner as the AAC user produces it sounds *worse*, not better, in nearly all evaluations. Each word is rendered with citation-form prosody (flat or falling pitch, terminal lengthening), producing a sequence of micro-utterances rather than a sentence. Pirinen & colleagues note in the broader incremental TTS literature that this is precisely the failure mode neural incremental synthesis was developed to avoid [7], [8]. The exception is when the AAC user explicitly wants a "running narration" register (e.g. dictating instructions, naming items) — a mode worth supporting as an option, not a default.

### (b) User-facing keystroke / word echo (auditory feedback to the speaker): well-established

A separate audio channel — typically routed to the user's earpiece or rendered at low volume — that speaks each character or word as it is selected is a *standard* feature of letter-based AAC systems and is documented to improve typing accuracy and reduce error rates, particularly for users with visual impairment or fatigue [9]. This is private feedback for composition, not output to the listener. It can use eSpeak-NG without any quality concern — it is functioning as a peripheral tactile-equivalent signal, not as speech.

### (c) The actually correct solution: incremental neural TTS

The modern answer to "speak naturally as the user composes" is **incremental TTS with lookahead**, not word stitching. Ma et al. [7] introduced a prefix-to-prefix framework that synthesises speech in an online fashion, achieving constant (1–2 word) latency with naturalness comparable to full-sentence synthesis. Saeki et al. [8] extended this with a pseudo-lookahead generated by a pretrained language model, removing the need to wait for the actual future context. The mechanism is fundamentally different from concatenation: the neural acoustic model produces continuous spectral frames informed by predicted (not yet typed) downstream context, so prosody is correct even mid-utterance.

The practical obstacle: incremental TTS is implemented only for high-resource languages (English, Chinese, Japanese, German) in the published literature. Adapting it to Afrikaans would require either retraining MMS-TTS-afr in an incremental decoding configuration, or fine-tuning an incremental TTS model on a smaller Afrikaans speech corpus. This is a research project, not a feature; mention only to clarify what "speaking as the user composes" properly looks like.

### A pragmatic middle ground: clause-level incremental commit

Without retraining anything, an effective compromise is **clause-level submission**: the system flushes to TTS automatically at every clause-terminating cue (comma, semicolon, full stop, or a manual "speak partial" button). Each clause is synthesised as a complete prosodic unit, so within-clause prosody is preserved; the listener experiences shorter chunks but each chunk is coherent. This addresses the conversational dead-air problem at much coarser granularity than word stitching, and works with any underlying TTS engine including the cache-first hybrid architecture above.

## On incremental TTS with lookahead — what is actually shippable for Afrikaans

The published work I cited [7], [8] is research code, English/Chinese/Japanese only, not packaged for deployment, and not retargetable to Afrikaans without retraining. To clarify the terminology — three distinct things are often conflated:

| Concept | What it does | Available for Afrikaans? |
|---|---|---|
| **Streaming TTS** | Synthesise full sentence; emit audio chunks as they're decoded. Reduces time-to-first-byte. | Edge-TTS yes; XTTS-v2 yes (but no Afrikaans); MMS-TTS yes. |
| **Incremental TTS** | Synthesise on partial input as it arrives, with explicit or pseudo lookahead for prosody. | No. Research only [7], [8]. |
| **Unit-level commit** | Wait for a user-defined unit boundary (word, comma, clause), then synthesise that complete unit. | Yes — implementable on top of any TTS. |

For your stated AAC use case, **unit-level commit is the right primitive**. It works with any backend (Edge-TTS, eSpeak-NG, or a hypothetical fine-tuned Piper). The granularity becomes a user-configurable setting:

- `commit_on = "word"` — speak each word the moment whitespace is entered. Highest perceived responsiveness, lowest prosody, equivalent to your original word-stitching idea but using actual TTS per word rather than concatenation of cached words. **For Afrikaans, this still mangles compounds** — *kindertehuis* typed and committed as three words *kinder*, *te*, *huis* is wrong; the user must type it as a single token to be spoken correctly. The system should treat anything between whitespace as one unit, so a typed compound stays whole.
- `commit_on = "punct"` — speak on `,`, `.`, `?`, `!`, `;`. Better prosody. Suitable when the user is composing longer thoughts.
- `commit_on = "submit"` — speak only on explicit Enter. The classical AAC pattern.

A radio-button or three-state toggle on the main screen lets the user switch modes per conversation.

## Predictive-text engine for Afrikaans — concrete options

Three rungs of increasing capability:

**1. **2. Small local LLM (mobile/desktop CPU, offline, ~1–4 GB).** Quantised Gemma-2-2B, Phi-3-mini, Qwen2.5-1.5B-instruct, or AfroLlama (specifically multilingual-African-language-fine-tuned). **3. SpeakFaster paradigm (LLM-powered abbreviation expansion).** Cai et al. The cited work was English-only with fine-tuned LLMs, but the architecture is language-agnostic.

For your stack, an interesting hybrid is: n-gram LM for the always-available always-fast baseline; local LLM as the suggestion engine when on Apple Silicon; cloud LLM (Claude/GPT) for the highest-quality SpeakFaster-style expansions when online and the conversation has context.

## The 30-day cache warmup is achievable, but the maths matter

Let $U_t$ be the number of unique utterances seen by day $t$ and $r$ the average daily count of utterances produced. Empirical AAC corpora suggest a heavy-tailed Zipfian distribution: a small core vocabulary dominates, with novel utterances arriving at a decaying rate. Roughly,

$$
U_t \approx U_\infty \left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)
$$

with $\tau$ in the order of 10–20 days for a stable user. Cai et al. [1] report eye-gaze AAC users producing roughly 5 000 utterances over 130 hours of observation across 6 months, of which a Zipfian core of 200–500 phrases accounts for the majority of speech acts.

If the system caches *every* unique utterance as 32 kbps mono MP3 (a defensible quality for speech), each second of audio is approximately 4 KB. A 3-second average utterance is ~12 KB. For an asymptotic $U_\infty = 2\,000$ unique utterances:

$$
S_\text{cache} \approx 2\,000 \times 12 \text{ KB} = 24 \text{ MB}
$$

This is trivial on any device. The cache storage is not the constraint; **network availability during the warmup is**.

Which leads directly to the most important architectural recommendation:

## Offline fallback in the browser

## Storage architecture: IndexedDB with persistent permission

The Cache API has a 50 MB hard limit per partition on mobile [7]. IndexedDB uses a quota that is a percentage of free disk (~6–60% depending on browser and persistence status) [8]. The decisive call is:

```js
const persistent = await navigator.storage.persist();
```

Without this, the browser may evict the entire origin's data under storage pressure or user inactivity — destroying the cache the user has spent 30 days building. With `persist()` granted, IndexedDB and Cache Storage are protected until the user explicitly clears site data [9]. The call should be made on first launch, with a brief UX explanation of why the permission is being requested.

Schema for the single IndexedDB object store, keyed on `utterance_hash`:

```
{
  utterance_hash: string,        // primary key, SHA-1 of normalised text
  text: string,                  // original utterance text
  audio: Blob,                   // MP3 audio, 32 kbps mono
  tts_source: 'azure' | 'gcloud' | 'espeak' | 'seed',
  play_count: number,
  last_used_ts: number,          // epoch ms
  created_ts: number,
  pinned: boolean
}
```

Indexes on `last_used_ts` (for LRU eviction within app-controlled bounds), `play_count` (for upgrade prioritisation), and `tts_source` (for the background-upgrade pass when the network returns and we want to replace `espeak`-rendered entries with neural renders).

## Predictive-text engine, recalibrated for low-end Android browser

Local LLMs in the browser via WebLLM or `transformers.js` are unrealistic on this hardware. A 1.5B parameter quantised model needs ~1 GB of RAM resident plus several hundred MB of model weights to download, and inference latency on ARM Cortex-A55 will be measured in seconds per token. Off the table.

Realistic options:

1. **N-gram language model in WebAssembly.** KenLM [10] compiled to WASM, with a trie-compressed Afrikaans 4-gram model trained on NCHLT and SADiLaR corpora. Model size with 8-bit quantisation is in the 10–50 MB range. Inference is sub-millisecond per prediction. This is the right primary engine.

2. **Cloud LLM for SpeakFaster-style abbreviation expansion**, online only. When the user has connectivity and types `k j v m k` (Afrikaans: "kan jy vir my koffie"), a Claude or Gemini API call expands to the most likely full phrase given conversation context. 3. **A static phrase-completion dictionary** built from the seed corpus and the user's accumulated phrase bank. Lowest-tech, highest-reliability, complements the n-gram for short prefixes.

The three combine: the suggestion strip shows the union of (a) cached/pinned phrase matches, (b) n-gram next-word predictions, and (c) — only when online and the user opts in — LLM expansions.

**3. Opportunistic background sync.** Register a Service Worker `BackgroundSync` event. The user experiences quality improvement over time without noticing the upgrade. Background Sync API support on Android Chrome is reliable; on AOSP-without-Google-services tablets, fall back to a foreground sync on app open.

Together, these reduce the meaningful cold-start window from 30 days to roughly day-zero coverage of common utterances, with quality improvements happening invisibly thereafter.

## Predictive-text engine for the browser

Rule out anything requiring a local LLM on this hardware. Two layers:

**Always-available baseline: 4-gram Kneser-Ney LM in IndexedDB.** Train on NCHLT Afrikaans text corpus [8] + Taalkommissie corpus + Afrikaans Wikipedia. Serialise to a compact binary trie (~20–40 MB), load lazily into IndexedDB on first run. Query latency on low-end ARM: sub-10 ms per next-word prediction. Implementation: a custom JS port, or compile KenLM to WASM (more work but well-trodden). This gives the 40–60% keystroke savings Trnka et al. demonstrated [9].

**Online enhancement: cloud LLM for abbreviation expansion.** When online, send the conversation context + the user's partial input to a small fast model (Claude Haiku, GPT-4o-mini, or Gemma-2-2B served via a CF Worker) and offer SpeakFaster-style abbreviation expansion [10]. Critically: this is *additive* — the n-gram baseline always provides suggestions; the cloud LLM provides better ones when reachable. Never block the UI waiting for the LLM.

For Afrikaans-tuned models specifically, AfroLlama and Inkuba-LM cover Afrikaans among other African languages; check their quality on representative AAC inputs before committing to one over a base multilingual model.

## YAFWOTs specific to this delivery model

- **Service Worker eviction.** Chrome will evict service workers and storage under pressure unless `navigator.storage.persist()` has been requested *and granted* (it can be silently denied). On day-one the SPWA must request persistence and the UI must surface failure if denied. Without persistence, a single low-storage event can wipe the audio cache and the 30-day re-warm starts over.
- **Audio autoplay gesture requirement.** All browsers block audio playback until a user gesture. The SPWA design must ensure the first play happens off a user tap (e.g., the "speak" button), not autoplay on page load.
- **Google TTS af-ZA installation friction.** Tier 2 requires the user (or deployer) to navigate Android Settings → System → Languages and input → Text-to-speech output → Google → Install voice data → Afrikaans. This is a one-time setup but easy to forget. The SPWA's onboarding must check `speechSynthesis.getVoices()` for `lang === 'af-ZA'` and walk the user/deployer through installation if absent, with screenshots.
- **WebView vs Chrome.** If the SPWA is wrapped in a WebView (e.g., via Trusted Web Activity for Play Store distribution), Web Speech API availability and OPFS quotas differ from standalone Chrome. Test on the actual target device, not just desktop Chrome devtools.
- **Building a custom n-gram trie before checking presage.** The `presage` library has been the AAC-community standard for two decades; check its model format and Afrikaans data availability before writing a custom trie loader. Even if the runtime needs to be JS-native, presage's data formats and corpus selection are well-worth inheriting.

## The Web Speech API caching subtlety

`speechSynthesis.speak()` plays audio directly to the device speakers and does not expose the synthesised waveform. There is no portable way to capture it for storage in OPFS [4]. The naïve consequence is that Tier 2 utterances never enrich the cache, so a user who stays offline for weeks accumulates no quality improvement on repeated phrases.

The clean fix is to decouple **what is logged** from **what is captured**:

- Every committed utterance is logged in IndexedDB regardless of which tier serves it — `(utterance_text, hash, last_used_ts, play_count, tts_source)`.
- On any cache miss, the dispatcher checks tier preference and serves audio.
- A `BackgroundSync` worker, when the device next has connectivity, iterates over log entries where `tts_source != 'edge'` AND `play_count ≥ k`, requests Edge-TTS synthesis for each via the Cloudflare Worker, and writes the resulting audio to OPFS. The next offline session for that phrase upgrades silently from eSpeak to Edge-TTS quality.

No audio capture needed. The log entry is the cache-warming signal. This pattern is also better-behaved on power and bandwidth: warming is opportunistic and batched, not a synchronous side effect of every utterance.

```
                              │ FastAPI / uvicorn → POST   │
                              │ JSON {text} → audio/opus   │
                              └────────────────────────────┘

   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │ TIER 3: Web Speech API → eSpeak (af)     │
   │ Fallback if Termux service unavailable   │
   └──────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Tier ordering, in dispatch priority:
1. **Cache HIT** — instant, any state
2. **Edge-TTS** — only if online; highest quality; populates cache
3. **Termux sherpa-onnx** — offline neural; medium-to-good quality; populates cache (now possible because we control the audio bytes)
4. **Web Speech API eSpeak** — offline formant; robotic floor; fallback if Termux service down

The introduction of Tier 2 (Termux) is the only material change. With it operational, novel offline utterances get neural-quality audio, the cache is populated even when offline, and the role of Edge-TTS narrows to "best-quality reference for cache warming when bandwidth is plentiful" — possibly only at provisioning time. **You may be able to remove the Cloudflare Worker from the runtime entirely**, keeping it only as a desktop-side tool the deployer uses to bulk-warm the cache. This eliminates a class of operational concerns: no rate limits, no API brittleness, no third-party dependency.

## SPWA ↔ Termux integration: the actual mechanics

1. **Inter-app communication is HTTP over `127.0.0.1`.** Chrome on Android can fetch `http://127.0.0.1:8001` from a page served from any origin, with CORS configured on the FastAPI side (`Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *` for development; restrict in production).
2. **Service availability detection.** The SPWA pings `GET /health` on the Termux service at startup and caches the result for the session. On failure, the dispatcher silently degrades to Tier 3.
3. **Cold start.** Python + uvicorn + model load takes 5–15 seconds on a low-end ARM. Service must be already running when the SPWA opens. Two viable patterns:
   - **`termux-services` (runit-based)** + **Termux:Boot** add-on: services start automatically at device boot, daemonised. The user never sees Termux.
   - **Persistent foreground notification** via `termux-notification --ongoing`: keeps Android from killing the process under memory pressure.
4. **Audio response format.** Server-side, encode VITS output to Opus at ~24 kbps — small enough to transfer over loopback in milliseconds, plays directly via Web Audio API in the SPWA.
5. **Cache population from Tier 2.** Unlike Tier 3 (Web Speech API, opaque), Tier 2 returns raw audio bytes. The SPWA writes these to OPFS keyed by utterance hash — every offline neural synthesis enriches the cache.

## Could a local LLM replace cloud LLM suggestions?

In principle, llama.cpp on Termux runs quantised models. In practice, on a low-end Android tablet with 2–3 GB RAM:

| Model | Quantisation | Size | RAM during inference | Tokens/sec on low-end ARM | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Qwen2.5-0.5B-instruct | Q4_K_M | ~400 MB | ~600 MB | 8–15 | Borderline viable for short next-word predictions |
| Gemma-2-2B | Q4_0 | ~1.6 GB | ~2.2 GB | 3–6 | Will OOM-kill the SPWA on a 2 GB device |
| Phi-3-mini-3.8B | Q4_K_M | ~2.4 GB | ~3.2 GB | 2–4 | Not feasible |

Qwen2.5-0.5B is the only one that has a chance. Even then, its Afrikaans capability is questionable — you'd want to evaluate held-out Afrikaans perplexity before depending on it. For v1, **stick with the n-gram model in the browser** for predictive text; reserve a Termux-hosted local LLM as a later experiment if the n-gram baseline turns out to be the binding constraint on input speed.

## Honest staged-adoption recommendation

You have two real choices:

**Option 1 — design for Termux from v1.** Architecture above is built around it. Provisioning gains: F-Droid install → Termux install → `proot-distro install ubuntu` (or native sherpa-onnx compile) → install the Piper-afr ONNX model → enable boot-start services → standard SPWA onboarding. Adds 30–60 minutes to provisioning per device but delivers neural offline TTS immediately.

**Option 2 — ship SPWA + eSpeak first; add Termux tier when warranted.** v1 deploys as in the previous architecture (cloud + cache + native eSpeak via Web Speech API). After 2–4 weeks of real use, measure cache hit rate and collect specific user complaints about offline quality. If eSpeak floor is the binding complaint, add the Termux tier as a non-breaking upgrade — the dispatcher accepts new tiers without other components changing.

If your end user is yourself or another technical person, Option 1 is fine. If the device is for someone non-technical (relative, friend, clinical context), Option 2 is more honest about the maintenance burden Termux imposes. Termux is not on the Google Play Store anymore (removed in 2020 due to policy issues around binary inclusion); a factory reset wipes it; an Android OS update can break the runit service auto-start; the user cannot diagnose any of this. The deployer becomes the on-call engineer.

## YAFWOTs

- **Trying to `pip install torch` on Termux.** Will fail. The Ubuntu-chroot build-from-source path technically works but is multi-day, fragile, and produces a binary that may not survive the next PyTorch release.
- **Skipping `proot-distro` and assuming ONNX Runtime wheels work on Termux native.** The wheels target manylinux glibc; Termux uses a non-standard libc layout. `proot-distro install ubuntu` is the standard workaround.
- **Running the Termux Python service without `termux-wake-lock`.** Android will doze-kill it within minutes of screen-off, breaking the offline neural tier silently.
- **Starting the Termux service from the SPWA's UI thread.** Not possible — there's no browser-to-Termux IPC for app lifecycle control. The service must be self-starting via Termux:Boot.
- **Depending on MMS-TTS-afr without verifying it exists.** Run the `curl -I` check on a real internet connection before committing to that path; default to Path A (fine-tune Piper on openSLR-32) as the safer assumption.
- **Bundling a local LLM in v1.** On a low-end tablet, the only viable model is Qwen2.5-0.5B at Q4, with questionable Afrikaans capability and 8–15 tokens/sec. The n-gram + (when-online) cloud LLM split is more realistic until you've measured what the user actually needs.

## Implications of "mostly online at home"

A few things that mattered when offline-first was assumed become non-issues:

1. **The 30-day cache warmup concern dissolves.** With Edge-TTS as the dominant runtime path, every novel utterance is rendered at high quality on first speak. The cache stops being a quality-floor mechanism and becomes a latency-and-cost optimisation. A user could conceivably stay online forever and never experience a Tier 2 invocation; the cache just makes repeated utterances instant.
2. **Per-word commit + cache works in your favour, fast.** Because each word is its own dispatcher invocation, the cache fills with the user's vocabulary rather than their sentences. Vocabulary's Zipfian distribution is steeper than sentences'; the most-used 200–300 words cover the bulk of conversational language [1]. Cache hit rate on individual words climbs much faster than it would on whole utterances — probably 80%+ within a week of regular use rather than the 30 days I was modelling for utterance-level caching.
3. **Provision-time cache warming becomes nice-to-have, not essential.** A short starter list of immediate-need phrases (names of household members, regular medications, emergency utterances) is still worth pre-warming so first-use is instant. Probably 30–50 items at most, not 500–1000. Five minutes of deployer time, not an hour.
4. **Background sync becomes optional.** With Tier 2 rarely invoked, there are rarely eSpeak-rendered cache entries waiting to be upgraded. The sync worker can be omitted from v1 and added later if usage patterns warrant it.

## What the per-word + Edge-TTS combination is actually doing for prosody

Worth being explicit about this since it informs the UX expectation: when the dispatcher operates on individual words, *every TTS engine produces citation-form prosody*. Edge-TTS Adri Neural saying "ja" still says it in isolation, with falling pitch and terminal lengthening — exactly the same prosodic shape as eSpeak saying "ja", just with much better timbre and segmental quality. So the value Edge-TTS delivers in this mode is **voice quality**, not sentence prosody. The chained-words effect (sequence of micro-utterances rather than a sentence) will be present regardless of which engine renders each word. You've accepted this trade-off; I'm just stating it plainly so you can defend it if a user later complains "it doesn't sound like a person speaking sentences."

If at some point a user does want full-sentence rendering for longer outputs, the dispatcher can be extended with a `commit_on = "submit"` mode that holds the buffer and synthesises the whole sentence as a single Edge-TTS request. This is a UI toggle, not an architectural change.

## Provisioning checklist, shortened

1. Install F-Droid client.
2. Install `com.reecedunn.espeak` from F-Droid.
3. Android Settings → Accessibility → Text-to-speech output → Preferred engine = eSpeak.
4. Open the SPWA. Onboarding probes `speechSynthesis.getVoices()` (with `voiceschanged` listener), confirms `af` is present, requests `navigator.storage.persist()`.
5. Optional: deployer pastes 30–50 immediate-need phrases into the warm-up tool, which fires them at the CF Worker and stores results in OPFS.

Steps 1–3 take 5 minutes. Step 4 is automatic. Step 5 is optional and takes another 5 minutes.

## Keeping the door open without paying for it

The single architectural discipline worth maintaining for possible future Termux/neural-offline addition: **make the TTS dispatcher tier-pluggable**. Define an internal interface that any tier implementation satisfies:

```ts
interface TTSTier {
  name: string;
  available(): Promise<boolean>;
  synthesize(text: string, lang: string): Promise<ArrayBuffer | null>;
}
```

`null` means "I cannot serve this; try the next tier". Edge-TTS, Web Speech API, and any future Tier 2.5 (Termux sherpa-onnx, ElevenLabs, fine-tuned Piper served from anywhere) all satisfy this interface. Adding a tier becomes a single-file change. The cost of this discipline is zero — it's the natural shape of the code anyway.

That's the whole v1 design. The rest is implementation.

## What replaces eSpeak as the failure-mode UX

Silence on tap is a worse UX than robotic speech. If you drop eSpeak, you must explicitly design what happens when audio cannot be produced. The honest minimum:

1. **Connectivity indicator in the chrome of the SPWA.** A small visible online/offline status icon. When offline and the user composes a novel phrase, they know in advance the speak-out path may fail.
2. **Visual "would speak" fallback.** On cache miss when offline, the composition surface enlarges the typed text and displays it prominently — large, high-contrast, possibly with a "show partner" button that maximises it. The text is on the screen; the listener can read it. This is functionally a digital version of pointing at written words on a card. Not great, but not a total failure.
3. **Queue and speak on reconnect.** When the network returns, queued utterances can be optionally spoken aloud, useful for "I was trying to say this earlier."

These three things are good UX additions regardless of whether eSpeak ever joins the stack — the offline indicator and text-fallback should exist anyway. So building them is not work you'd save by including eSpeak; you'd build them either way.

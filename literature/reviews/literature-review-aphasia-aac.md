## 1. Epidemiology of Post-Stroke Aphasia

### 1.1 Global Prevalence and Incidence

Aphasia is among the most prevalent acquired communication disabilities. Approximately
one-third of stroke survivors develop aphasia — the RELEASE Collaborators (Brady et al.,
2021) confirmed this figure across a multilingual international dataset of 943–1056
participants: "approximately one-third of the 25.7 million stroke survivors worldwide
experience aphasia" [PMC:read]. This figure aligns with the Beukelman (2020) report
that "the median proportion of stroke patients described as having aphasia is 34%,"
with "approximately 100,000 individuals discharged from US hospitals annually" carrying
an aphasia diagnosis following left-hemisphere CVA [MKV:read].

Global prevalence of aphasia in the UK has been estimated at approximately 350,000
stroke survivors living with aphasia at any one time [web-snippet, BMJ Evidence-Based
Nursing, 2022 commentary on RELEASE]. Koul (2011) reports UK prevalence of
"about 66 per 100,000 people" [MKV:read].

Frederick et al. (2022, *Perspectives of the ASHA Special Interest Groups*,
DOI: 10.1044/2022_PERSP-22-00111) conducted a systematic review of the global rate
of post-stroke aphasia and noted that existing prevalence data is skewed toward
high-income countries — making the sub-Saharan African evidence base sparse [abstract].
The South African stroke burden is substantial but under-documented in the aphasia
literature. Mitchell et al. (2020, *Aphasiology*, DOI: 10.1080/02687038.2020.1759772)
reported inpatient prevalence from a UK population-level study: of 1,948 consecutive
stroke survivors, 39% had aphasia on admission, 31% at discharge, with 37% having
dysarthria as a co-occurring disorder [abstract]. These inpatient figures suggest
the working figure of 30–40% is robust across healthcare settings.

### 1.2 Age and Gender Profile

The population with post-stroke aphasia skews older. Beukelman (2020) reports that
"most people who have aphasia acquire it after the age of 60 years," though a
"substantial number of younger adults" are also affected [MKV:read]. In AAC-specific
studies reviewed by Koul (2011), the mean participant age was 63.73 years (range
27–86) [MKV:read]. The RELEASE meta-analysis found that younger age (<55 years)
was a significant predictor of greater language recovery — conferring a +15.4-point
improvement on the Western Aphasia Battery Aphasia Quotient (WAB-AQ) versus older
participants [PMC:read].

Gender distribution is approximately equal in the reviewed populations: Koul (2011)
reports 48% male, 52% female in AAC outcome studies [MKV:read].

### 1.3 Chronicity and Long-Term Burden

Long-term language impairment affects a large proportion of stroke survivors.
The RELEASE meta-analysis reported that "long-term language impairment affects 61%
of stroke survivors' communication at 1 year after onset" [PMC:read].
Beukelman (2020) reports that "up to 40% of all people with aphasia will experience
chronic, severe language impairments across modalities" — this is the population
for whom AAC is clinically indicated [MKV:read].

Koul (2011) notes that time post-onset in AAC outcome studies ranges from 8 to 106
months, and that this variable does not predict AAC outcome: "Time post-onset brain
injury had no impact on successful outcome" in computer-based AAC use [MKV:read].

## 2. Neurological Basis of Post-Stroke Aphasia

### 2.1 Left-Hemisphere Language Network Damage

Aphasia is typically caused by damage to left-hemisphere perisylvian language areas
following ischemic or haemorrhagic stroke. The classical Wernicke-Lichtheim model
identifies Broca's area (left inferior frontal gyrus, Brodmann areas 44/45) as
critical for language production and Wernicke's area (posterior superior temporal
gyrus) as critical for comprehension [web-snippet, *Brain*, 2018]. More recent
neuroimaging work has complicated this picture: lesion-symptom mapping studies show
that Broca's aphasia requires damage not only to Broca's area but also to underlying
white matter tracts, particularly the superior longitudinal fasciculus [web-snippet,
*Frontiers in Language Sciences*, 2025].

Koul (2011) provides the standard taxonomy based on Boston/WAB classification,
organising aphasia types around the fluent/nonfluent distinction [MKV:read]:

| Aphasia type | Fluency | Comprehension | Characteristic errors |
|---|---|---|---|
| Global | Severely nonfluent | Severely impaired | Near-absent output |
| Broca's | Nonfluent, agrammatic | Mild–moderate impairment | Telegraphic; effortful |
| Transcortical motor | Nonfluent | Mild impairment | Initiation difficulty |
| Wernicke's | Fluent (jargon/paraphasias) | Severely impaired | Paraphasias, neologisms |
| Transcortical sensory | Fluent | Severely impaired | Echolalia; preserved repetition |
| Conduction | Fluent | Mild–moderate impairment | Severely impaired repetition |
| Anomic | Fluent | Preserved | Word-finding difficulty |

The participant in this project presents with non-fluent expressive aphasia, intact
comprehension, and functional literacy — a profile consistent with
**Broca's or transcortical motor aphasia**, both of which involve anterior
(left frontal/premotor) lesions.

### 2.2 Co-Occurring Disorders: Agraphia, Apraxia, Alexia

Aphasia is not a single-modality impairment. Koul (2011) identifies the standard
co-occurring disorders: "Aphasia is frequently associated with concomitant disorders
affecting motor speech functions (e.g., dysarthria or apraxia of speech), sensory
recognition (agnosia), reading (alexia), writing (agraphia), and swallowing
(dysphagia)" [MKV:read].

**Agraphia** is the specific writing disorder co-occurring with aphasia.
Thiel, Sage and Conroy (2016) documented four agraphia subtypes in post-stroke
aphasia [MKV:read]:

1. **Surface/lexical agraphia**: impaired orthographic lexicon; over-reliance on
   phoneme-to-grapheme conversion; produces phonologically plausible but orthographically
   incorrect spellings ("serkle" for *circle*, "clok" for *clock*).
2. **Phonological agraphia**: impaired phoneme-to-grapheme conversion; unable to spell
   nonwords or unfamiliar items; proper nouns, place names, and new vocabulary become
   effectively unspellable.
3. **Deep agraphia**: semantic-to-orthographic pathway damaged; semantic errors in
   writing ("dish" for *spoon*); specific difficulty with verbs and abstract words.
4. **Graphemic buffer disorder**: short-term orthographic holding mechanism impaired;
   produces addition, substitution, omission, and transposition errors scaling with
   word length ("stemp" for *stamp*, "dace" for *dance*).

Thiel et al. (2017) noted that "writing is particularly sensitive to brain damage
due to its inherent complexity in incorporating linguistic, cognitive, perceptual
and spatial processes" [MKV:read]. Critically, switching from handwriting to keyboard
does not bypass this impairment: "Handwriting requires knowledge of letter shapes and
the grapho-motor skills to produce letters, whereas to select letters on a keyboard
visual recognition skills and spatio-motor are important" — but the central linguistic
bottleneck (lexical retrieval, phonological encoding, orthographic sequencing) remains
unchanged [MKV:read].

The lived experience of writing impairment is profound. Thiel and Conroy (2022)
interviewed eight people with post-stroke aphasia and writing difficulties, finding
that participants described severe subjective distress: "the spelling is so hard and
also it's the pain in my life"; "I read them and I don't, I read them I can't I don't
write them"; "Sentences are completely different" [MKV:read]. This asymmetry between
preserved reading ability and impaired writing production is the defining feature of
why literacy alone does not justify a keyboard-based AAC design.

**Apraxia of speech** (AOS) frequently co-occurs with anterior aphasia syndromes
(Broca's, global, transcortical motor), disrupting motor execution of even residual
speech. Limb apraxia can additionally impair pointing, gesturing, and device
operation [MKV:read: koul-2011-aac-aphasia, beukelman-2020-aac].

### 2.3 Language Recovery: Mechanisms and Predictors

The RELEASE meta-analysis (Brady et al., 2021) is the largest individual participant
data meta-analysis of aphasia recovery to date — 174 research studies, 943–1056
participants across four language domains [PMC:read]. Key findings:

- **Earlier intervention is crucial**: Enrollment within 1 month of onset was
  associated with the greatest recovery across all domains: +19.1 WAB-AQ points
  (overall language), +5.3 correct on Token Test (comprehension), +11.1 Boston
  Naming Test points (naming), +1.1 AAT Spontaneous-Speech Communication subscale
  (functional communication). Each association was significant at 95% CI.
- **Age predicts recovery**: Younger age (<55 years) was associated with +15.4
  WAB-AQ points, +6.1 Token Test, +9.3 Boston Naming Test, +0.8 Spontaneous-Speech.
  "Improvements in language scores from baseline diminished with increasing age
  and aphasia chronicity."
- **Recovery continues past 6 months**: "Recovery continued to be observed to a
  lesser extent beyond 6 months poststroke" — contradicting older views that chronic
  aphasia is static. Spontaneous recovery is indeed greatest in the first 3–6 months,
  but therapeutic gains are achievable beyond this.
- **Sex was not a significant independent predictor** in the multivariable analysis.
- **Multilingual dataset**: The dataset was explicitly multilingual and international,
  suggesting these recovery patterns apply across language backgrounds.

Neuroimaging studies (not yet in MKV corpus) have identified three recovery
mechanisms [web-snippet, *Nature Reviews Neurology*, 2019]:
1. **Perilesional tissue engagement** — spared tissue adjacent to the lesion
   compensates for damaged areas.
2. **Right hemisphere homologue recruitment** — right-hemisphere language
   homologues activate, particularly in cases with extensive left-hemisphere damage.
3. **Degenerate network use** — domain-general networks (executive control,
   working memory) contribute to language processing through alternative routes.

The relative contribution of each mechanism depends on lesion location and extent.
For anterior lesions producing Broca's/transcortical motor profiles, perilesional
recruitment and right-hemisphere homologue engagement are the most documented
recovery routes.

## 3. Aphasia Classification and AAC Candidacy

### 3.1 The Garrett–Lasker–Beukelman Communicator Framework

The six-level communicator classification (Garrett & Lasker, 2005; Garrett &
Beukelman, 1992), as described in Beukelman (2020) and operationalised in
Lowis et al. (2024/CAYA) and Koul (2011), is the clinical standard for AAC
candidacy determination in aphasia [MKV:read: all three sources]. The framework
defines communicator levels by what the person can do independently, not by
impairment severity:

| Level | Independence | Defining capability | Literacy requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Emerging | Partner-dependent | Inconsistent yes/no response | Very limited |
| Contextual choice | Partner-dependent | Points to written/pictorial choices in context | Recognise a word in context |
| Transitional | Partner-dependent | Accesses stored messages with cueing | Recognise personally relevant text |
| Stored-message | Partner-**independent** | Independently accesses pre-stored messages | Word/phrase/sentence level |
| Generative | Partner-**independent** | Generates novel messages via letters/symbols | Functional; phonological strategies |
| Specific-need | Partner-**independent** | Mostly functional; breaks down in high-demand situations | Generally functional |

### 3.2 Functional Assessment Tools

The primary candidacy assessment instrument is the Multimodal Communication Screening
Task for Persons with Aphasia (MCST-A; Garrett & Lasker, 2005), which "effectively
distinguishes between communicators who require partner support to indicate choices
and stored-message AAC communicators who can independently search through the
booklet to locate symbols" [MKV:read: beukelman-2020-aac]. The Western Aphasia
Battery-Revised (WAB-R) and Cognitive Linguistic Quick Test Plus (CLQT+) provide
severity and cognitive profiling respectively.

The CAYA framework (Lowis et al., 2024) operationalises candidacy into five questions
covering: unaided strategies; partner-supported strategies; external stored information;
stored messages via speech output; generative messages [MKV:read].

### 3.3 Profile of the Project Participant

The participant — non-fluent expressive aphasia, intact comprehension, functional
literacy at the word/phrase/sentence level, limited fine motor control, no prior
touchscreen experience — maps to the **stored-message communicator** profile:

- Intact comprehension enables him to understand questions, follow conversation,
  and navigate a labelled display without partner curation.
- Literacy at word/phrase/sentence level is the defining competence of stored-message
  communicators per Beukelman (2020) and CAYA [MKV:read].
- Non-fluent expressive profile means spontaneous speech production is severely
  limited — pre-stored messages substitute for the damaged production pathway.
- Partner-independent initiation is achievable: unlike transitional communicators
  (who need cueing), he can independently select messages in familiar contexts.

## 4. Aphasia Rehabilitation: Speech and Language Therapy

### 4.1 Effectiveness of Speech–Language Therapy

Aphasia rehabilitation is effective. The RELEASE meta-analysis found that "focused
therapeutic intervention may benefit people with chronic aphasia" and that "recovery
continued to be observed to a lesser extent beyond 6 months poststroke" [PMC:read].

Odendaal and Tönsing (2024) reported that South African speech-language pathologists
"agreed that both restorative and compensatory approaches are essential, and many use
AAC from the outset with their clients" [MKV:read]. This reflects international
consensus that restorative SLT and compensatory AAC are complementary rather than
competing interventions.

The Cochrane review on speech and language therapy for aphasia (*Brady et al., 2016*,
not yet in MKV corpus) found evidence for SLT effectiveness across intensity and
duration, with high-intensity therapy producing greater gains — though evidence quality
was rated as moderate due to heterogeneity in study design [web-snippet, not directly
verifiable from MKV].

### 4.2 Intensity and Dosage

The RELEASE meta-analysis identified enrollment timing (<1 month post-onset) as the
single strongest predictor of language recovery [PMC:read]. This finding drives the
clinical recommendation for early SLT initiation. However, for persons with chronic
aphasia (>6 months post-onset), intervention remains beneficial — a position supported
by the Tobii aphasia guide ("AAC applies to anyone with aphasia, any time post their
onset") [MKV:read: tobii-aphasia-therapy-guide].

### 4.3 Multimodal and Technology-Assisted Therapy

Pierce et al. (2019) conducted a systematic scoping review of multimodal aphasia
treatment, distinguishing between interventions aimed at improving "total
communication" (the AAC model) versus improving a single modality [MKV:read:
pierce-2019-multimodal-therapy]. Their review identified two key dimensions:
aim (total communication vs. single modality) and modality combination. AAC
approaches for aphasia explicitly target total communication rather than
single-modality production.

Weissling and Prentice (not dated in MKV, but cite key communication strategies)
document multimodal input strategies for non-SLP practitioners, confirming that AAC
is routinely integrated with formal therapy rather than treated as a post-therapy
intervention [MKV:read: weissling-aphasia-communication].

Huang et al. (2021) conducted a randomised controlled trial protocol for
AAC-augmented inpatient SLT, using a stored-message communication board with
"45 pictures relating to basic needs, emotional expression, medical conditions, and
daily activities" as the primary intervention. They report that "AACT may
simultaneously strengthen communication by reducing the pressure to retrieve target
concepts using impaired language functions" — explicitly framing AAC as augmenting
rather than replacing SLT [MKV:read: huang-2021-aac-inpatient-rct].

## 5. AAC for Post-Stroke Aphasia: Evidence Base

### 5.1 Core Clinical Evidence

The evidence base for AAC in post-stroke aphasia is well-established across several
decades of research, consolidated in Beukelman and Light (2020) Ch. 15 and Koul and
Beck (2011) [MKV:read: both].

Key findings from the AAC outcome literature:

**Symbol access and recognition:**
- "People with aphasia with minimal reading ability on formal tests were more than
  90% accurate when responding to conversational questions by pointing to written
  choices, as long as the choices were presented within the context of a conversation
  and spoken aloud by the partner" [Beukelman 2020, MKV:read]. This finding
  establishes that recognition-based AAC achieves high accuracy even in users who
  would formally test as having near-zero reading ability.
- Concrete over abstract symbols: Beck and Fritz (1998), reviewed in Koul (2011),
  found that "across all participants, a significantly fewer number of iconic codes
  were learned for abstract messages than for concrete messages" [MKV:read].

**Display design:**
- Petroi, Koul and Corwin (2014) established that "people with aphasia identify
  symbols within an AAC device most readily when fewer symbols per screen are
  displayed and when minimal navigation is required" [Beukelman 2020, MKV:read].
- Working memory and navigation: multi-step navigation imposes significant
  processing load on users who demonstrate problems recalling message locations.
  Low-density, single-level displays reduce this burden [Beukelman 2020, MKV:read].

**Symbol combination:**
- Koul (2011): "all participants with Broca's aphasia and one participant with
  global aphasia were able to identify and combine graphic symbols to produce
  sentences of varying degrees of syntactical complexity" [MKV:read]. Broca's
  profiles can engage in symbol-combination tasks — suggesting the project
  participant may be able to move toward more generative features over time.

**Technology-specific outcomes:**
- Formica et al. (2024) document a case series including a 71-year-old stroke patient
  whose profile parallels the project participant — "writing capabilities were also
  damaged but verbal comprehension was preserved... communication was based on the
  patient's selection and choice of images shown on the computer screen" — and whose
  selection-based AAC succeeded where production-dependent alternatives had failed
  [MKV:read: formica-2024-aac-brain-injury].

### 5.2 AAC Does Not Impede Speech Recovery

Families often express concern that using AAC will substitute for natural speech
recovery. The evidence directly contradicts this:

- Tobii aphasia guide: "Using AAC will not hinder PWA's ability to regain speech.
  Instead, it can help PWA get back into their social circles and daily activities
  helping to prevent depression" [MKV:read].
- Huang et al. (2021): "previous studies have reported that people with aphasia and
  their families often reject to adopt AAC strategies with the fear that AAC may
  interfere with or impede the restoration of their natural language system. However,
  studies have shown that AACT may simultaneously strengthen communication" [MKV:read].
- Odendaal and Tönsing (2024): "both restorative and compensatory approaches are
  essential" — South African SLPs confirm the integration model [MKV:read].

### 5.3 South African SLP Context (Odendaal & Tönsing, 2024)

The Odendaal and Tönsing (2024) study on South African SLP perspectives provides
direct contextual relevance. Key findings [MKV:read: odendaal-2024-aac-aphasia-slp]:

- South African SLPs follow the Garrett–Lasker framework: "in general... appeared
  to follow the principles of Garrett and Lasker's functional classification
  framework, initially focusing on aspects such as unaided AAC... once the person
  with aphasia is open to alternative forms of communication, they introduce more
  comprehensive AAC systems."
- Generic systems do not work: "Many noted that generic systems do not work for
  this population and that AAC systems must be highly personalized to benefit them."
  One participant stated the system must be "as meaningful as possible to that
  person."
- Communication partner is essential: "You need that golden thread of the
  communication partner... If you do not have that, then your aphasic patient will
  struggle to be an independent user of the AAC device."
- Low technology access: participants noted challenges with low device availability,
  limited family/caregiver support, and inconsistent AAC follow-up in South Africa.

The Odendaal (2022) study on South African SLTs more broadly documented systemic
resource constraints in the South African healthcare context that affect consistent
SLT provision for people with aphasia [MKV:read: odendaal-2022-sa-slts].

## 6. Psychosocial Impact of Aphasia

### 6.1 Depression, Anxiety and Emotional Well-Being

Post-stroke aphasia has a severe psychosocial impact. The RELEASE meta-analysis
explicitly notes that "aphasia is associated with poorer performance on... emotional
well-being after stroke" [PMC:read]. Beukelman (2020) documents the case of Marco —
a stored-message communicator whose barrier to AAC success was not cognitive but
motivational: "he was ashamed of using the machine in public" [MKV:read].

Tobii (2023) states that restoring communication through AAC prevents depression:
devices "can help PWA get back into their social circles and daily activities helping
to prevent depression" [MKV:read].

Depression post-stroke aphasia is a well-documented comorbidity in the broader
literature (not yet fully represented in the MKV corpus). The double burden —
language impairment plus emotional distress at the loss of communication — is
clinically important. Thiel and Conroy's (2022) interviews confirm the emotional
register: "the spelling is so hard and also it's the pain in my life" [MKV:read].

### 6.2 Social Isolation and Participation

Aphasia causes dramatic contraction of social networks. Beukelman (2020) identifies
"the individual's social networks have become so limited that there are few
opportunities to communicate" as a documented AAC failure mode — the intervention
itself cannot succeed if the social ecology that motivates communication has
collapsed [MKV:read].

Tobii's needs assessment guide emphasises that functional communication extends
beyond medical contexts to social participation: family conversations, community
engagement, personal expression [MKV:read: tobii-aac-needs-assessment].

### 6.3 The Caregiver and Communication Partner

The caregiver is a co-participant in aphasia. Beukelman (2020): "research shows that
when a PWA has at least one close supporting person they will have a significantly
greater chance of successfully living with aphasia. Failure to identify and adequately
prepare communication partners is a common reason for unsuccessful AAC intervention
among people with severe aphasia" [MKV:read].

Odendaal and Tönsing (2024) document this with the "golden thread" framing:
the communication partner is "as much of a ramp or a crutch as an eye gaze device
is for someone with motor neuron disease" [MKV:read]. For the project, the primary
caregiver (Mom) is the essential co-user of the system.

### 6.4 Motivation and Self-Determination

Personally relevant vocabulary is a documented success predictor. Fox, Sohlberg and
Fried-Oken (2001), as cited in Beukelman (2020), showed that "one individual
communicated much more extensively about topics that he had selected than those that
had been chosen for him" [MKV:read]. TouchChat's clinical guide confirms:
"There is no standardized vocabulary file that will meet even the minimum needs of
every person with aphasia... Customisation is the responsibility of the
speech language pathologist" [MKV:read].

## 7. Afrikaans-Specific Considerations

### 7.1 Afrikaans as a Minority Healthcare Language

Afrikaans is spoken as a first language by approximately 6.2 million people in South
Africa (2022 census; ~10.6% of the population) and is one of 12 official languages.
However, it occupies a minority position in healthcare settings where English and
Zulu/Xhosa populations dominate services. This creates specific challenges:

- **No commercially available Afrikaans AAC vocabulary set**: Hattingh et al. (2020)
  documented the development of Afrikaans core vocabulary for AAC — confirming that
  standard Afrikaans AAC content does not exist off-the-shelf [MKV:read:
  hattingh-2020-afrikaans-vocab]. This justifies the custom PWA approach.
- **TTS voice availability**: Edge TTS `af-ZA-WillemNeural` is used in the project,
  providing a male Afrikaans voice — one of very few TTS systems supporting Afrikaans.
- **Limited SASL Aphasia research**: The peer-reviewed literature on aphasia in
  Afrikaans speakers is sparse. The broader South African SLP context is documented in
  Odendaal and Tönsing (2024) and Bornman et al. (2025) [MKV:read], but
  Afrikaans-specific aphasia data is absent from the major databases.

### 7.2 Multilingual Aphasia: General Principles

While Afrikaans-specific data is unavailable, the broader multilingual aphasia
literature provides relevant principles. The RELEASE meta-analysis explicitly
included a multilingual, international dataset, suggesting that demographic recovery
patterns generalise across language backgrounds [PMC:read]. The dataset's "language
of assessment" variable was included as a covariate but not found to be a significant
independent predictor of recovery magnitude in the multivariable analysis.

In bilingual/multilingual aphasia, it is established that (a) the two languages
may be differentially impaired, (b) recovery may be faster in the dominant
language, and (c) assessment in the correct language is essential for valid
diagnosis. For the project participant, Afrikaans is his dominant language and the
language in which he reads — making Afrikaans the only appropriate language for both
assessment and AAC vocabulary.

### 7.3 TTS and Afrikaans AAC

The project uses Microsoft's `af-ZA-WillemNeural` voice. This is to date the
highest-quality Afrikaans neural TTS voice available in any commercial or open system.
Its use in a PWA delivers offline-capable speech output from stored Afrikaans text —
a capability that did not exist before approximately 2022. This represents a genuine
technical gap being filled by the project.

## 8. AAC Technology Adoption: Older Adults and Touchscreen Unfamiliarity

### 8.1 Barriers to AAC Acceptance

A consistent theme across the clinical literature is that AAC adoption depends on
factors beyond device capability. Beukelman (2020) documents failure modes specific
to person-system fit:
1. Vocabulary mismatch: content does not match real-life communication opportunities.
2. Partner rejection: communication partners unwilling to accept AAC.
3. Physical form factor mismatch: device too large/unwieldy or too small for vision.
4. Social network collapse: few remaining opportunities to communicate.
[MKV:read: beukelman-2020-aac]

The case of Marco (stored-message communicator, Beukelman 2020) illustrates the
social acceptance dimension: "he was ashamed of using the machine in public." This
shame-based rejection is not a device failure but a social reintegration failure —
addressable through partner training and community reintegration support [MKV:read].

TouchChat (2024) adds technology familiarity as an adoption factor: "when the
person with aphasia has not been a major technology user in the past, keep that in
mind when getting them started" [MKV:read: touchchat-2024-aphasia-guide].

### 8.2 Large-Target, Low-Density Design for Unfamiliar Technology Users

For older adults and technology-naive users, user interface complexity is a
documented adoption barrier. The intersection of three independent constraints
for the project participant — aphasia-specific cognitive load, post-stroke fine
motor impairment, and zero prior touchscreen experience — converges on the same
design prescription: large targets, single-level navigation, minimal interface
chrome.

The AAC evidence on display density (Petroi, Koul & Corwin, 2014; Wallace & Hux,
2013, both reviewed in Beukelman 2020) establishes that aphasia-specific working
memory and navigation constraints require this design independently of the motor
access argument. The motor and experiential constraints compound the aphasia-specific
evidence [MKV:read].

The CAYA clinical guide (Lowis et al., 2024) formally includes "navigation ability —
number of levels on a display" as a dimension of candidacy assessment for stored-
message communicators [MKV:read].

### 8.3 Online AAC and Offline Requirements

The project PWA operates fully offline — a critical design decision for the South
African home context. Vodafone/Vodacom AAC research notes document the mobile data
cost burden and reliability challenges affecting AAC access in South Africa
[MKV:read: vodafone-group-aac-research, vodacom-aac-research — cross-referenced
to notes/ rather than literature/mkv/].

Huang et al. (2021) note that in-patient AAC using a paper-based communication board
was preferred over digital systems in moderate-to-severe aphasia because of simplicity
and accessibility — suggesting that the design principle of minimal interface
complexity outweighs the affordances of high-tech systems for this population
[MKV:read: huang-2021-aac-inpatient-rct].

## 9. Design Science Implications for the AAC PWA

### 9.1 Why a Custom PWA Rather Than an Off-the-Shelf System

The convergence of three gaps justifies the custom PWA approach:

1. **No Afrikaans AAC system exists**: Hattingh et al. (2020) documented Afrikaans
   vocabulary for AAC [MKV:read] but no commercial system implements it.
2. **Generic systems do not meet the specific user's communicative ecology**:
   Odendaal and Tönsing (2024) and TouchChat (2024) both confirm the
   customisation imperative — vocabulary must reflect the user's actual life,
   people, and routines [MKV:read].
3. **Offline, touchscreen, large-target requirements**: no off-the-shelf system
   combines Afrikaans TTS, offline operation, and a large-target low-density
   interface suited to this user profile.

### 9.2 From Literature to Design Requirements

Drawing directly from the literature reviewed in sections 1–8, the following
design requirements are evidence-grounded:

| Requirement | Evidence basis |
|---|---|
| Stored-message model | Production vs. recognition asymmetry in aphasia; Broca's profile [Beukelman 2020; Koul 2011; Thiel et al. 2016/2022] |

## 11. Summary

Post-stroke aphasia affects approximately one-third of stroke survivors globally,
with up to 40% developing chronic severe impairments qualifying them for AAC.
The condition primarily affects the perisylvian language network of the left
hemisphere, producing not only impaired speech but agraphia, alexia, apraxia of
speech, and cognitive changes — making writing (including keyboard-based typing)
an impaired rather than spared pathway.

Recovery is possible and continues beyond 6 months, but earlier intervention
produces the largest gains. AAC and speech language therapy are complementary
interventions, not alternatives: the clinical consensus, supported by South African
SLP data, is that AAC should begin at onset.

The participant in this project — non-fluent expressive aphasia, intact comprehension,
functional literacy, touchscreen tablet user — maps to the stored-message communicator
profile on the Garrett–Lasker continuum. His literacy enables accurate recognition
from labelled displays; his production impairment (including agraphia) makes
keyboard-based typing an inappropriate primary interface. Stored-message AAC
routes communication through the preserved recognition pathway, achieving >90%
accuracy even in users who formally test as near-zero readers.

South African SLPs (Odendaal & Tönsing, 2024) confirm this framework: the
Garrett–Lasker model, the customisation imperative, and the central role of the
communication partner. The Afrikaans-specific gap — no commercially available
AAC vocabulary or system — provides the core justification for this project.
